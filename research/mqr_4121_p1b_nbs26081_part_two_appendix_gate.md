## Part Two pp.33–35: Operational precision is not chemical specificity
The text distinguishes precise optical transmission from a chemically selective analysis. It discusses cell cleanliness, handling and orientation, sample/reference alignment and temperature as contributors to observed discrepancies. In the experimental narratives, even the optical glassware positioning can dominate precision; repeating the same cuvette in consistent orientation reduces a class of geometric disturbance. Crucially, these cautions are about **measurement history**—not just a timeless numeric result. MQR state variable must include relevant instrument/sample preparation conditions when they affect future observations.

## pp.35–40: Chemical determination and interference
The report reviews chemical assays for several metals in diverse materials. It records interferents, competition/kinetics, time-dependent color development and techniques for discriminating target chemistry from non-target signals. The nickel example is instructive: signal evolution in the presence of another metal can make time of observation a relevant variable, and two-wavelength comparisons can diagnose or account for some cross-response. This is a measured chemical-specificity problem, not reducible to the stray-light problem alone.

## pp.40–43: Positive scientific method discovery
The authors consider greater selectivity through more complex chemical systems, and chromatographic separation before optical detection. Conceptually this is very important for MQR: instead of forcing a single scalar instrument readout to resolve chemically confounded species, change the experiment to make previously inseparable processes yield differentiated signals. Extra experimental operations may induce fresh disturbance or demand new calibration, but can expand the testable question family.

## Margin notes — MQR research additions
1. When time changes a colored reaction, observation y(t) has an intervention-relative state transition; two spectra compared at unequal response ages are not necessarily identical-measurand evidence.
2. A second wavelength is not automatically an independent physical observation channel: source, optics, calibration and preparation often remain common-mode, although spectral contrast can discriminate chemistry.
3. Chemical separation is not merely data post-processing; it changes the measurement architecture and may yield newly testable hypotheses.
4. Distinguish what an instrument validates (transmission/wavelength) from what chemistry validates (selectivity/equilibrium/species) and what history validates (stable, comparable material).
5. There are at least three sources of curvature in MQR P1A: instrument spread/stray, measurement bandwidth, and changing chemical composition or slow kinetics. Apparent absorbance curvature is not itself mechanism identification.
