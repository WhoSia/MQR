## What changed as the models changed

1. **1855–1856.** The problem was to give a mathematical expression to relations already pursued through Faraday's lines of force. The imaginary fluid supplies a representation. Maxwell's methodological introduction treats physical analogy as a partial relation between laws. Nersessian's account situates this move in Faraday's experiments and diagrams, Thomson's mathematical and physical analogies, and Cambridge continuum mechanics.
2. **1861, Parts I–II.** The question shifts toward a dynamics that could generate the patterns of force. The vortex model links rotational motion and directional stress, then raises a difficulty of its own. Adjacent vortices rotating in the same direction have oppositely moving surfaces at contact. An intermediate layer of counter-rotating particles supplies a conceivable connection. Its translation can represent conduction in the model. Maxwell explicitly calls this specific connection provisional at p. 486. The novelty of the problem about adjacent surfaces is a consequence of choosing the vortex model. It is not a new observed anomaly in the electromagnetic data.
3. **1862, Part III.** A persistent conduction current, static polarization, and a changing displacement now require distinct treatment. At pp. 490–491 Maxwell starts from electrical properties of conductors and dielectrics and describes a change of displacement as current-like before calculating with an elastic-cell model in Propositions XIII–XIV. Nersessian reads the model sequence as productive of the concept and the equations, drawing also on Siegel for the difficulty of open circuits. Chalmers grants that the mechanical construction helps connect an electrical constant to the transverse-wave speed in the early paper, but argues that the idea of displacement and its variation has an independent route from dielectric behavior and that the 1865 field paper gives the term a different role. The order of the published argument supports a distinction between proposing a phenomenon and giving it a model-specific quantitative role. It does not recover the private order of discovery. Nor does the 1862 sign convention or current equation license simply copying the later total-current interpretation back into Part III. The 1865 paper explicitly distinguishes conduction, displacement and their combined current.
4. **From a numerical comparison to a material question.** Part III, Propositions XVI–XVII, compares a wave speed inferred from Weber–Kohlrausch electrical experiments (310,740 km/s) with a value from Fizeau's optical experiment quoted in the main text (314,858 km/s). The relative difference using that optical value is about 1.31%; this is an arithmetic comparison, not an uncertainty analysis. Maxwell draws a physical inference and goes on to connect a dielectric's inductive capacity with its refractive index and magnetic inductive capacity (eq. 142). The theory now gives electrical and optical practices a joint question about materials. In the Treatise, arts. 788–789, Maxwell explicitly discusses dispersion and compares the square root of the measured dielectric capacity of solid paraffin (about 1.405) with a long-wavelength optical extrapolation from melted paraffin (about 1.422). He judges the discrepancy larger than observational errors. The different physical states and measurement regimes make this a historically instructive comparison, not a controlled material comparison by current standards.

## Return to MQR's question

In the [waveguide case](mqr_54_mqr41_waveguide_shared_measurement_reference_planes.md), an interface was built so that field calculations and measurements could address a specified terminal response. In the Maxwell case, constructing and revising the theory supplied a reason to compare measurements across domains that already had distinct practices. A map between established vocabularies captures only part of this achievement. A new representation can propose a comparison, and the comparison can expose gaps in the representation or in how its quantities are measured. One concrete case is the need to decide what it would mean to compare electrical and optical properties of the same dielectric under comparable conditions.

For 5.4, the question is now: **How do representations make a cross-domain comparison worth performing, and how do the resulting measurements reshape the comparison itself?** This keeps the origins in view: the division between observation and computation, the cooperation of different sciences, and the generation of questions. It also separates the cognitive-historical claim that a particular model produced an idea from the demonstrable claim that the published theory made a new comparison available. For the next reading, chapter 3 of Nersessian offers a contrasting source type: Clement's spring problem has a think-aloud protocol, whereas the Maxwell case relies largely on published reasoning and surviving records. Test what each can establish about the order in which a problem and its representation changed.

No lab measurements were taken. No claim of historical priority for the general idea of comparing electricity and optics is made.
