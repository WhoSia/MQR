## 1810 material-data transmission failure traced directly in original

1810 printed pp129–132: Guyton describes erroneous published alumina 0.25, silica 0.64, lime 0.06 (summing to 0.95; note that source did not give rest); attributed to Vauquelin in *Bulletin des sciences* Floréal an VII (= May 1799), reprinted *Annales des arts et manufactures* Frimaire an X (~Dec 1801), p302 and German Scherer's December 1799 periodical. In subsequent explicit correspondence Vauquelin rejected numerical attribution and reported fresh analysis of *Wedgwood supplied pieces*: silica **47.35**, alumina **44.29**, water **8.36**, summing **100.00** in that reported set. He found earlier notebook analyses approximately the same. This provides a substantive period first-person report in printed form, stronger than relying on Chaldecott 1975 paraphrase alone; but Vauquelin's autographed letter is still an **indirect quotation by Guyton**, and original 1799 journal and original notebooks NOT checked. Other contemporary source variations may exist.

Guyton p132–133 cites M. Gazeran who used a local clay, then added 63 parts silica; Guyton argues this inhibited the shrinkage a useful pyrometer specimen required, and drying/pressing compounded differences. He treats *tests of counterfeit recipe* as poor tests of the original tool: reproduced calibration requires controlling chemical/material recipe and manufacturing method, not merely following nominal temperature readings. He did not assert all criticisms were invalid.

## 1810 environmental confound: a pyrometer reflects actual local heat history, not furnace-wide heat

1810 pp134–138: a comparison of clay pyrometer indicators in the same furnace, measured against a water pot outside or at a different position, is not a like-for-like test of identical temperature. Local charcoal air flow, slag, geometry and flame direction change the heat experienced. Guyton recounts two specific reposition/control examples: one run gave Wedgwood ~64 degrees vs typical over 142, on improved fuel/air flow the same setting yielded ~139; another raised the indicator about **9–10 cm** in same furnace and saw reading **36°W** rather than **106°W** at customary placement. This is evidence for spatial heterogeneity of furnace heat, not yet proof of ideal instrument consistency. Text extraction is legible but reported precise figures and figure captions should be visually checked before treating them as quantitative raw microdata.

## 1814 scale replacement is not simply validation

1814 printed p115 rejects Wedgwood's 0°W ≈ 1077°F and 1°W =130°F, presenting revised approximately **0°W =517°F** and **1°W=62.5°F**. Guyton points to disagreement about **silver melting point** (Wedgwood believed 28°W, other observers ~22°W) and different instrument composition. His new calibration is based on mercury thermometry → **platinum expansion pyrometer** → clay pyrometer, and multiple metal fusion fixed points, explicitly printed p117. This is a **multi-instrument historical calibration strategy**, not present-day ITS-90 traceability or demonstrated invariant clay shrinkage.

1814 p236 Table no.3 lists later fixed-point coordinates (e.g. silver at 22°W, Fahrenheit ~1892.67; cast iron flowing 130°W, Fahrenheit ~8696.24; manganese melting 160°W, Fahrenheit ~10517.12), BUT Google OCR has extensively scrambled alignment, some rows and centigrade numbers contradict affine 517 + 62.5W; cannot assume either those table numbers are a single global affine model or reliably extracted. Printed p115 explicitly qualifies linear progression as a further assumption. The reconstructed p115 affine proposal is F(W)≈517 + 62.5W, historical Wedgwood F(W)≈1077.5 +130W; at W=160 these would be 10517 and 21877.5°F respectively (using rounded coefficients). **This is an illustrative consequence**, not a modern actual temperature and NOT a proof Guyton adopted a globally linear corrected table for every substance.

## Implications for MQR

Three historically independent challenges to naive validity: (A) primary material recipe was incorrectly published and reproduced by other manufacturers; (B) experiments presumed the 'same furnace' implies same local thermal history; (C) calibration bridges were revised through different physical indicator materials and fusion points. Therefore a critique that the Wedgwood high-temperature scale is inconsistent may confound **different physical objects, different thermal histories and different absolute scale assumptions**. To adjudicate whether it is a thermometer of temperature alone, need controlled same-piece, same heat-duration experiments, independent temperature indicators and transfer-of-procedure audits. This extends explicit primary evidence but source-theoretic insights overlap Chang 2004, Ohnesorge 2022, Chaldecott 1975; MQR novelty HOLD.
