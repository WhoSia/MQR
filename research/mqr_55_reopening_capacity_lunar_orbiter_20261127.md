## Minimal conditional discrimination argument
Let S be a scalar signal retained in a source, and old representation F=clip(S;0,1). For s1=1.1 and s2=1.4, F(s1)=F(s2)=1. A decoder seeing only F cannot distinguish them. If the preserved upstream record R is injective on these two states and a calibrated reader recovers R, the contrast can be restored. If R itself had clipped the states, changing downstream reconstruction cannot identify the contrast from R alone. This is elementary noninjectivity, not a new theorem; no numerical relation to actual tape density. In a stochastic model, distinguishability of induced distributions replaces pointwise injectivity. Recovery depends on reader calibration and recoverable source content; a raw tape alone is not a sufficient witness. Changing the decoder is not a new observation of historical target; two decoded outputs from one signal remain dependent in acquisition errors, though decoder-specific errors can be challenged independently. A fresh measurement at t1 is new world contact but estimates change relative to t0 only with alignment and photometric/geometry comparability. Equal observed image differences could arise from target change or acquisition transformation. This connects 0.43–0.47 without inventing a universal score.

## Cross-case result and counterexamples
Grinnell preserved observation structure intentionally; LOIRP reinstated access to upstream records after practical dormancy. Both constrain future scientific ability but differ in operational pathway. Direct loss of underlying signal is unlike reversible reader loss. A preserved reader with unrecoverable source is another counterexample. Functional substitution could reopen without original equipment; LOIRP does not establish exact restoration universally necessary.
