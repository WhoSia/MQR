## P0: endogenous observational selection

Let X denote a possible evidence world, A an institutional authority/selection policy, S_A(X) the records selected/retained by A, and O(X,A)=encode(S_A(X)) the actually accessible archive. Let J_k(X) be a claim-scoped warranted judgment in context k. The distinction between X and O(X,A) is mandatory.

**Fiber identification lemma.** For a fixed selection policy A, the observationally inferred J_k exists as a well-defined function of O_A(X) iff J_k is constant on each fiber of O_A. Proof: if J_k=j∘O_A then equal O values give equal J values; conversely choose their common J value for each observed record. This is a basic factorization criterion, not an original categorical discovery.

When A is unknown or authority varies, observational equivalence must be defined over pairs (X,A). Identifiability based on the *observed* pair (A,O) requires J_k(X) constant for all compatible (X,A) that induce that pair. If authority identity is unrecorded, only O remains and fibers are generally larger. Such loss of information cannot by itself strengthen identifiability.

### Finite countermodel

A transparent construction has worlds x_ind and x_shared with two visible citing papers and the same Greenberg-style citation arrows. In x_ind, independently authenticated provenance witnesses exist; in x_shared, the papers trace to the same experimental root. The observed citation table is identical under the same selector A; the claim-specific independent-evidence judgment J1 differs. Hence J1 cannot factor through this citation-only observation map. These are hypothetical hidden states, **not** reported actual dependence among Greenberg papers.

Separately, authorities A1 and A2 can select different subsets from the same world X, yielding differing degree statistics even if the underlying source-root graph stays fixed. Citation concentration changes may therefore result from the observation policy rather than any change in evidence quality.
