## Reading ledger and preliminary analytic results (based on full-book searchable text, investigated chapters, not exhaustive page-by-page illustration verification)

- Contents: Introduction pp.1–17; Ch.1 'Electricity and Galvanism in the Early Nineteenth Century' p.18; Ch.2 'Electromagnetism' p.52; Ch.3 'Ampère’s First Studies of Electromagnetism: Entering a New Field' p.85; Ch.4 'Competing Pursuits in Paris, 1820–1821' p.143; Ch.5 'Electromagnetism in London' p.202; Ch.6 'Faraday’s First Studies: Electromagnetic Rotation' p.228; Ch.7 'Experiment and Concept Formation' p.301; Appendices A/B p.339/384; Notes p.423; bibliography p.461.
- Introduction pp.1–4: final concepts used in retrospective publication may not have existed when the investigation began. Laboratory records, informal notes, sketches and communications can contain evidence lost in polished results. Research decisions depend partly on materials, institutions, time and resources; no general causal calculus of 'best choice' follows.
- Ch.3 opening pp.85–86: Steinle divides Ampère's electromagnetic entry into early three-week formative period before Sept 25, 1820, and more settled months through Jan 1821. His archival reconstruction identifies simultaneous loosely connected inquiries, including reduction to general facts, rather than a single pre-fixed theoretical aim.
- Ch.4 pp.143–144, 191–192: competition with Biot/Savart, publicity and failed measurement attempts matter in history; a decisive stabilization after Sep 25 should not be projected backwards to erase prior uncertainty. Evidence access differs before and after surviving lecture manuscripts.
- Ch.6 circa p.230: Faraday's surviving diary is a cleaned, orderly record possibly transcribed from lost provisional laboratory notes; Steinle explicitly *hypothesizes* its reconstruction rather than treating it as mechanically unedited data. The contrast with Ampère's original archival material cautions against uniform confidence in dated records.
- Ch.7 pp.301–337: treats experiment's conceptual/epistemic functions rather than merely hypothesis testing; argues early regularity discovery and stabilizing preliminary scientific language constitute positive epistemic work. pp.321–327 compare Gooding's `construals` and reduction to simple cases, and give comparative historical analysis. pp.332–337 discusses discovery/justification, conceptual formation, retrospective disappearance of formative work; at pp.337 he explicitly warns that epistemically exploratory circumstances do **not** compel every researcher to choose exploratory tactics. Biot's maintenance of a Laplacean framework remains a rival viable historical strategy.
- Provisional result for MQR: 'inquiry criteria change while being made' and 'unsuccessful/abandoned exploratory pathways are epistemically meaningful' have strong prior art in Steinle. This work already foregrounds contingencies, actor choices, rival strategies and archive provenance. A new MQR theory must explain an independent adjudication or demonstrate a stronger discriminating prediction, rather than repeat Steinle's narrative using new labels.
