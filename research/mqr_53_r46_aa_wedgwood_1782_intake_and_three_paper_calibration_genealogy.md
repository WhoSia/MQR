## 3. Close reading of an EXISTING full primary scholarly study

**Hasok Chang (2004), _Inventing Temperature: Measurement and Scientific Progress_**, ch.3 *To Go Beyond*, printed pp.118–155, already complete in Drive `1RwuRfY9Ifz6iEAUZZm57z4EzHX8CP0CV`. In the directly read chapter:
- pp.118–121: Wedgwood's problem is a high-temperature domain beyond reliable mercury thermometry, where performance as an *investigative tool* initially matters even without fully known conversion into the old scale. In a reported 1782 claim, shrinkage was taken to saturate rapidly and depend only on maximum thermal exposure; Chang notes later evidence challenges time independence.
- pp.123–126: the **1784 bridging scale** uses silver expansion to build a multi-link conversion to Fahrenheit. As Chang reconstructs it, a factor **20 Fahrenheit degrees per silver degree** and **6.5 silver degrees per Wedgwood degree** imply **130 Fahrenheit degrees per Wedgwood degree**. The 1784 initial red-heat point was estimated **1077.5°F**. THESE ARE WEDGWOOD'S HISTORICAL CALIBRATION CLAIMS AS TRANSMITTED BY CHANG, not modern valid temperature conversion constants.
- pp.125–127: 1786 material-preparation complications: consistency of clay, molding methods, alum mixtures and supplementary fixed points; the alleged stability of clay shrinkage and conversion to other scales have **different error sources and different evidential demands**.
- pp.128–141: Guyton de Morveau, platinum expansion and later pyrometers challenge Wedgwood, but the apparently independent standards are themselves model-dependent and not uniformly calibrated. Retrospective agreement of alternatives does not prove each alternative directly knows the true high temperature.

## 4. Target the genuinely difficult philosophical remainder, not Chang imitation

Do not collapse four distinct propositions:
`S`: repeatability of shrinkage readings for a fixed material recipe and firing history;
`F`: inter-batch reproducibility across clay-piece fabrication;
`C`: conversion of Wedgwood degree into Fahrenheit using silver and other standards;
`T`: identification of a unique *temperature* across different high-temperature processes.

Neither S⇒F, F⇒C nor C⇒T without separate source-supported model and domain assumptions. One observation y of shrinkage could depend on temperature T, exposure duration t, recipe/batch b and local atmosphere a: `y=f(T,t,b,a)`. **This is a minimal analytic possibility, not verified source functional form.** If the original claim that duration is negligible is relaxed, then estimating T uniquely from a single y generally requires independent information about t,b,a or constraints establishing invariances; otherwise nonidentification can persist. This is elementary inverse-problem logic, NOT an original mathematical theorem. Determine whether 1784 and 1786 proposed *actual tests* that eliminated the corresponding nuisance factors, and whether historian Chang evaluated those exact tests.
