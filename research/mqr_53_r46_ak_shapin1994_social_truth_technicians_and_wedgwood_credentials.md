## Shapin's argument summary: trust is necessary, not sufficient

Author overview XXV–XXX: direct experience has never made testimony and authority dispensable. Knowledge is a collective good, relying on judgments of other people as reporters; the author explicitly rejects the reading that trust is **all** that constitutes knowledge. He presents trustworthy personal identity as a necessary but NOT sufficient condition for stable empirical knowledge. Gentlemanliness is a culturally localized historical solution, not a universal current criterion or a claim about biological superiority.

## Ch.5 Epistemological Decorum (pp.193 ff)

Chapter examines how claims with uncertain ontological fit were handled in the 17th c: new telescopic/microscopic/maritime and artisanal discoveries challenged prior plausibility systems. Shapin explicitly asks what learned classes had known of potters, miners and chemical workers. Testing factual claims is socially managed amid uncertainty; rational acceptance is not a context-free algorithm. This is a strong pre-existing account of why new reports can deserve investigation before final truth is settled.

## Ch.7 Certainty and Civility (pp.310 ff), esp. printed pp.323–325

Shapin's historical point that 'Boyle did not write Boyle's law' is not the denial that Boyle observed air pressure-volume relationships. It is the warning that 17th-century Boyle did not formulate a modern universal algebraic **ideal-gas** law. Boyle assembled experimental readings from a J-shaped mercury tube, compared readings with a theoretically expected reciprocal relation, and evaluated degree of fit rather than literal identity. Translation to PV=k for ideal gases changes the represented referent and a historical actor's ontological universe; retrospective law-naming can obscure the historical fact/theory relation. Specific 17th century 'air' was not the modern ideal-gas kind.

## Ch.8 Invisible Technicians (pp.355 ff): Denis Papin

Shapin documents Boyle's 1680 air-pump work acknowledging remunerated assistant Denis Papin. Papin designed a modified air pump, performed and registered observations, and was involved in composing narratives. An author/person bearing the final published credibility may not be the sole empirical/technical producer of the work. Shapin flags that a proposed asterisk system marking Papin's own experiments was not actually implemented. Any inference from published author name to all experimental labor is a category error.

## Critical intersection with MQR Wedgwood

Shapin's evidence concerns primarily seventeenth-century gentlemanly scientific testimony, whereas Wedgwood 1780–1786 is a later potter/manufacturer, FRS and court supplier. The 1784 printed title is 'By Mr. Josiah Wedgwood, F.R.S. Potter to Her Majesty' (vol.74 1784 catalog OCR https://jimclifford.ca/philosophical-transactions-ocr-1665-1869/volumes/74/). This combination of learned-society membership and publicly asserted professional material expertise is an interesting *credential configuration*; not independent proof of its causal role in committee approval. Nor may we assume that he was socially classified as outside gentlemanliness. Compare acts of credibility: (A) claimed empirical reproducibility/instrument expertise, (B) personal standing and trade/court title, (C) affiliation F.R.S., (D) selection as worth printing.

Important contemporaneous control within **same 1784 volume**: besides Wedgwood, Philosophical Transactions 74 includes Cavendish's Experiments on Air, Kirwan's Remarks, Cavendish's Answer, Kirwan's Reply. The issue did not require a permanent single approved answer to host reasoned debate; still, sequence/dates and outcome require original text study, and publication of controversy is not itself proof there was no editorial judgment.
