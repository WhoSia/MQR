# MQR 5.4 — Medicine, epistemic capability and the historical formation of occupational authority
2026-11-27 KST | MQR 46 | comparative historical research, not a new version.

## Root return
Previous macro synthesis and rational-retention research distinguish scientific claims, capabilities, and criteria. The user's new comparison of Joseon medicine and European/American medicine exposes a further distinction: epistemic authority (warrant for a claim), occupational jurisdiction (legal authority to practice), social prestige, and material capacity (institutions/resources) are not interchangeable. Medical history is a comparative case family within all seven founding questions, not a replacement for them.

## Transition in Korea
Hwang (2024), https://doi.org/10.3352/jeehp.2024.21.36, full HTML selected sections https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11894032/:
- late 1880s vaccinator licensure (activity-specific exclusion of unlicensed practice);
- 1899 Euihakkyo; 1902 first 19 graduates as reported by Hwang;
- 1900 doctor regulations included both Western and traditional doctors; school graduation/examination routes;
- 1913 colonial regulations, effective Jan 1914, differentiated modern doctors and traditional euisaeng; imperial jurisdiction restricted some licenses;
- 1951 National Medical Law and 1952 first national physician examination; separate qualification categories; 1962 law removed tier classification and established legal dualization of medicine and traditional medicine.
Hwang describes shortage of healthcare resources and public/legislative resistance to excluding traditional doctors. Kim (2020) https://doi.org/10.31791/JKH.2020.03.188.1 abstract adds wartime/occupation/post-liberation manpower and administrative changes. Actual original legal texts and archived enrollment/pay/prestige data not independently collated; do not claim a measured causal history of modern status.

## Europe and America: distinct pathways, not universal linear sequence
Pinell (French original 2009, DOI 10.3917/rfs.502.0315; English translation 2011 volume, DOI 10.3917/rfs.525.0117) abstract/indexed text https://shs.cairn.info/article/E_RFS_502_0315/pdf?lang=en. France after 1795: clinical medicine, social medicine and auxiliary sciences organized around different institutions; hospital clinicians dominated, but cross-field hybrids expanded prevention, knowledge and societal authority. This is directly relevant prior art to MQR capability recombination and institutional authority. French Revolution reorganized medical/surgical education; not simply medicine descended from barbers. The 1803 two-tier professional scheme is a lead, not full law audit.
UK 1858 Medical Act official enacted text index https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Vict/21-22/90/enacted: recognized qualifications and registration. Roberts (2009), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2629176/, abstract/indexed text, political mediation among occupational and lay interests. Registration does not imply instant exclusion of every unregistered practice.
Flexner (1910), Medical Education in the United States and Canada, Carnegie foundation 1960/1972 reproduction, https://archive.carnegiefoundation.org/publications/pdfs/elibrary/Carnegie_Flexner_Report.pdf. Selected introduction and chapter XIV pp.180–181 extracted directly. Laboratory education and school standards reform coexisted with explicit racially differentiated expectations; seven Black medical schools, five judged unable to contribute effectively, two recommended for continuation. Report recommendation, not estimated causal effect. Screenshot retrieval failed; no page-image parity. ## Comparative hypothesis and falsifiers
Distinguish scientific competence K, scope-specific evidential warrant W, occupational jurisdiction J, material/educational capability R, public prestige P and realized patient/public access A. These are dimensions, not commensurate quantities or an identified causal model. Candidate feedback: R enables K and feasible inquiry; institutions allocate R and confer J; successful public outcomes may increase legitimacy, demand and resources; J may also preserve or restrict who can practice, learn or contribute. Professional prestige cannot by itself certify a treatment, and absence of legal monopoly does not imply absence of expertise. No claim that institutional factors explain all medical development or that medicine's modern clinical efficacy is merely socially constructed. Preserve ethical safeguards, patient benefit and independent evidence.

Discriminating test: compare actual dated changes in qualification, legal scope, admission/access, research techniques, clinical outcomes and social prestige separately. Joseon 1463 differentiates incentives without measuring efficacy; Korea 1900/1913/1951 shows rules changing scope without implying identical underlying knowledge; Flexner shows standards and exclusion coexisting, but causal attribution requires rival mechanisms. Compare more inclusive institution with same competency safeguards; historical counterfactual not identified. No prescriptions about present-day physician supply or treatment, no quantitative cross-era prestige inference. Professionalization sociology (Starr, Pinell, Roberts) and historical practice theory (Kitcher, Kuhn) already explain much; MQR's distinct research question is whether institutional evolution can generate/erase epistemic capabilities and cross-practice bridges, beyond merely reallocating occupational authority.
