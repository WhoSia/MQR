## A finite decision rule

Within one run, suppose detector output is a stable unknown function of intensity and the noiseless value must lie in each reading's supplied interval. For every candidate, intersect the intervals belonging to equal predicted intensities. An empty intersection rules out the candidate under even an arbitrary single-valued detector. Sort the distinct predicted intensities. At every later group, its upper limit must be at least the maximum lower limit of previous groups. Require a strict inequality for strictly increasing detectors. For sufficiency in the weak case, the running maximum is a feasible response. For the strict case, if there are two or more groups, choose a positive step no larger than the least ratio of ordered upper-minus-earlier-lower gap to index difference, and propagate the maximum of the current lower bound and the preceding response plus that step. The resulting increasing assignments can be interpolated. The finite criterion guarantees existence, not an empirically established response curve. Standard ordinal invariance motivates the model. [Stevens 1946](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.103.2684.677).

Observed drop 110 rules out predicted constant intensity 111 with one stable detector, even without knowing its gain. A flat 111 record can fit both physical candidates if saturation is possible. A truly separate blocked/full reference with disjoint intervals removes that explanation for the ideal 0/1 predictions. For intermediate predictions, the endpoints need not resolve anything. The response 1/2 can fit latent 1/4 and 3/4 under different strictly increasing detector curves with the same endpoints. An exact independent middle anchor (1/2,1/2) excludes both for that response. If the measured output lies strictly below the middle reference, only the low predicted intensity can survive in the constructed example. The analysis assumes stable measurement conditions and trusted intensity constraints on anchors, with no bound on detector slope. An incompatibility also puts shared preparation and detector stability at risk if those assumptions lack independent support.

## Public empirical check and source files

Rivera-Ortega, Barcelata-Pinzon and Saldaña-Gonzalez (2025) print five sensor readings at each of seven angles in Table 2. All 35 printed readings were transcribed. Neighboring observed ranges do not overlap and are ordered as predicted by Malus's law on 0–90 degrees. The means at the two endpoints are 60 and 2.4, in reported sensor-reading units. Subtract the latter and divide by the difference to make an illustrative endpoint-scaled output. At 45 degrees the resulting value is 0.4375, 0.0625 below the ideal 0.5. This is the largest absolute mean residual among the five intermediate angles in this table. The crossed-polarizer endpoint is a low transmission condition and cannot serve by itself as a separately measured dark current. The paper notes dependence on sensor linearity and environmental light. At 45 degrees, the listed five observations give population SD 0.80 or sample SD 0.8944, whereas the table prints 0.73. Without the author's calculation method the printed SD is not adopted as an uncertainty bound. Neither order of the two different operator types, an independent detector calibration, or a second detector appears in this table. The published data support a one-apparatus ordinal check. A physical translation test still requires matched preparation, both distinct module orders and independent references. Include blocked/full and a reference between the two predeclared expected signals where possible. Randomize or counterbalance the order of runs, and alternate optical references with test runs to distinguish a real reversal from changing source or detector response.
