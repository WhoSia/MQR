## Source-dated target changes
1957 paper explicitly proposes measurement of molecular weight and partial specific volume; discusses density and mass heterogeneity, bands, calibration and independent composition determinations by chromatography. Reports density differences smaller than .001 g/cm^3 detectable under stated DNA/CsCl circumstances, not a universal detection limit. 1958 introduction explicitly motivates development of density-label discrimination for tracking parental atoms; publication order does not establish an independent accidental invention of the technique or a unidirectional causal sequence. 1958 Fig.2 reports light/heavy DNA band separation corresponding to .014 g/cm^3. No inference of precision from the ratio .014/.001, since the latter is a reported capability rather than calibrated uncertainty.
1958 discussion pp.676–678 reports one half-labeled species at generation one, equal quantities half-labeled and unlabeled at generation two; authors infer two conserved nitrogen-bearing subunits and one parental subunit per daughter under present conditions. Their Watson–Crick section explicitly withholds the claim that each identified subunit is a single polynucleotide chain or that the measured molecules are individual Watson–Crick molecules. The observable distinguishes specified replication patterns, not every possible microscopic mechanism. The difference between total nitrogen-label partition and connectivity/sequence is a logical scope distinction: total label fraction is unchanged by rearrangements that preserve label counts, if all other forward-model variables are fixed. Actual buoyant density also depends on molecular composition and structure, so do not assert arbitrary real rearrangements produce exactly equal band profiles.
1958 pp.679–681 heat-denaturation discussion and Fig.9 captions report E. coli hybrid material separating into two density species in equal amounts, apparent molecular weight approximately halved, density difference .015 g/cm^3. Comparison with separately labeled material supports dissociation and continuous conserved subunits; authors reject Delbrück's particular alternative. The same paper reports salmon-sperm DNA retaining apparent molecular weight despite density increase, and explicitly discusses alternative structural interpretations. This is a contrast between reported preparations, not a modern claim about species-wide DNA strand behavior, nor an independent modality: shared density-gradient technique and model assumptions remain. No proof that such questions had never previously been articulated: Watson–Crick model already motivates strand-level hypotheses.
