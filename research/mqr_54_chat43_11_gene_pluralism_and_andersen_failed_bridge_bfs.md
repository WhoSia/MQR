## 1. Gene concepts: stable scientific inquiry through changes in what a term picks out

MacLeod (2012) argues that describing classical-gene history as frozen, disconnected definitions obscures how working geneticists maintained continuous research questions and methods despite radical explanatory change. He follows scientific work from early character/factor accounts through chromosomal and biochemical reinterpretations; their concepts were *open-ended investigative resources*, not static vessels for a timeless list of properties. The claim is not that every historical definition referred to the same absolutely identical object.

Brigandt (2012) distinguishes **reference**, **inferential role** and **epistemic aim**. The classical gene supported prediction and statistical explanation of inherited patterns across generations; molecular genetics developed questions about the mechanisms and products of genetic material. Later biological findings made a simple bijection between DNA regions and functional products untenable in many contexts. Alternative splicing can yield different products associated with one underlying region; trans-splicing and overlapping products complicate the reverse attribution. Researchers concerned with RNA products, protein products, or different functional targets may use different criteria for gene individuation.

This is **not** the assertion that any redefinition is equally warranted or that genetically distinct objects necessarily have no common physical ancestry. Brigandt's positive thesis is that a *specific epistemic goal* and empirical inferential success can justify variation in what counts as a gene. MacLeod emphasizes continuity of open inquiry; Brigandt gives a more articulated account of why changes can be rational even when referents and goals change. These are not identical philosophical frameworks and should not be collapsed.

**MQR countermodel:** If different scientists both call something a gene, an absolute one-to-one referent dictionary need not exist. The legitimate operational translation may instead be a **goal-indexed partial relation** between material regions, products, and experiments. A toy bipartite one-to-many/many-to-one graph illustrates possible failure of a bijection, but must **not** be passed off as the biochemical structure of a particular gene or a formal discovery about all biology.

## 2. Andersen: a historically documented challenge that initially failed to cross disciplines

Andersen (2012) analyzes collaboration between physicists and chemists over new physical phenomena in the 1930s, with distinct but **interlocking** taxonomies. Different experts needed one another's results to make inferences and decide what the measurements might mean. Her 1934 Ida Noddack discussion illustrates that a chemically motivated alternative could be rejected in part because its advocate did not adequately work through the physics community's constraints or build an interactional bridge. Later developments required collaborators to link apparently contradictory reports with different disciplinary explanatory resources.

This is **Andersen's interpretation of the episode**, not a newly verified account of each historical participant's private beliefs; do not claim that Noddack established the later fully developed theory or that rejection had a unique cause. The historical details of hazardous physical experiments are **not** a procedural template for MQR.

Crucially, the observed science was not saved by possessing a universal translator in advance. It required a jointly investigable **bridge object**, experts' mutual calibration of trust, awareness of neighboring explanatory constraints, and revision of existing distinctions. Yet not every failed cross-disciplinary communication is evidence of incommensurability: domains can simply ask unrelated questions.

## 3. Four-case BFS synthesis with explicit counterevidence

| Case | Epistemic change | Stronger-than-needed inference to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Nersessian CAT (record07) | A research group develops an investigative feature while changing physical/computational practices | A derived visualization always adds new physical sensor bits |
| Steinle magnetic poles (record09) | A useful concept loses explanatory primacy for some research aims | The older concept becomes empirically false for every application |
| MacLeod–Brigandt genes (this record) | Concept reference and purpose vary across coherent scientific research contexts | Lack of unique meaning makes scientific communication impossible, or licenses all meanings |
| Andersen interdisciplinary case (this record) | An anomalous proposal has difficulties crossing expert boundaries; later coordination revises linked categories | The eventual outcome proves that early participants already possessed the later full explanation |

**Comparison target:** Different science vocabularies may be productive and even rationally stable without one global dictionary. But the precise claims each vocabulary licenses must be challenged in domains where they overlap and could disagree with a common physical outcome. An absent cross-field correspondence is not itself a world-observed difference.

It is a restricted framework for comparing scientific inquiry under **goal-indexed partial translations** and **world-contact-derived revisions**, emphasizing cases of productive divergence and failed communication rather than selecting only successful historical outcomes.

## 5. New question and backtrack capsule

How can two scientists, who individuate objects differently and pursue different epistemic goals, jointly formulate a falsifiable *third* question without presupposing an external universal ontology? An empirical answer would require a concrete shared measurement or intervention in which their rival concept applications predict **different outcomes**, and a record of how they negotiate what the result licenses.
