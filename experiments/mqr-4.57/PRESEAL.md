## Frozen admissibility obligations

### S1 — Agent and role declaration
The ecology must expose which agents can propose challenges, report costs, define targets, score proxies, allocate attention, fund work and ratify results.

### S2 — Private-information surface
Material private information about cost, competence, feasibility, target dependence or payoff must be represented as potentially strategic rather than presumed truthful.

### S3 — Incentive map
Every authority-bearing report must have an explicit account of how the reporting agent benefits or loses under the institutional rule.

### S4 — Supply-endogeneity audit
Observed challenge supply may not be treated as exogenous when incentives affect which challenges are generated, withheld, split, bundled or relabeled.

### S5 — Cost-report challenge
Reported cost must be distinguishable from realized cost and social opportunity cost.

### S6 — Target-framing challenge
Agents must be able to manipulate or contest how the operative scientific target is framed; target choice cannot be assumed incentive-neutral.

### S7 — Proxy-arbitrage audit
If several admissible proxies/metrics exist, strategic selection among them must be visible.

### S8 — Performative feedback audit
The policy must model cases where applying a ranking/funding/evaluation rule changes future behavior, data distribution, challenge supply or target semantics.

### S9 — Coalition/common-mode audit
Multiple nominally independent agents may coordinate, share incentives or converge on the same exploitable proxy.

### S10 — Anti-Sybil / multiplicity discipline
Creating many nominal challenge suppliers or challenge variants may not automatically create independent epistemic weight.

### S11 — Mechanism counterfactual
At least one alternative incentive or allocation mechanism must be available as a counterfactual audit surface when feasible.

### S12 — Exterior/randomized challenge reserve
A live ecology must retain at least one challenge route not allocated entirely by the incumbent strategic mechanism.

### S13 — Equilibrium ceiling
Strategic or performative stability may not be promoted directly to epistemic adequacy.

### S14 — Mechanism-local optimizer ceiling
A mechanism may be locally optimal under declared agent utilities and information assumptions without becoming a universal institution for science.

### S15 — Endogenous-incentive reopening
Evidence that agents adapt to exploit the live constitution must be capable of reopening `M`, `Ω` or both.

### S16 — Capture attribution
When performance degrades, the receipt must distinguish world/model failure from mechanism-induced selection, reporting or supply effects.

## Frozen countermodel bank

### CM1 — Challenge withholding
A high-value challenge threatens a dominant agent's claim or career payoff, so the agent strategically withholds it while supplying weaker alternatives.

Pressure:
observed challenge supply is not an exogenous frontier.

### CM2 — Challenge stuffing
An agent floods the ecology with many low-cost, nominally distinct challenges that all favor its preferred target framing.

Pressure:
challenge count is manipulable.

### CM3 — Cost shading
Two agents face the same true cost, but one strategically reports a lower cost to win attention/funding.

Pressure:
reported opportunity cost is not social opportunity cost.

### CM4 — Cost inflation / barrier creation
An incumbent overstates the cost of an exterior challenge to protect the incumbent lane.

Pressure:
high reported cost can be strategic exclusion.

### CM5 — Target relabeling
After an adverse result, an agent narrows or shifts the declared target so the same result becomes “irrelevant.”

Pressure:
target drift can be strategic, not merely natural.

### CM6 — Proxy shopping
An agent chooses among admissible evaluation metrics after seeing which one ranks its challenge highest.

Pressure:
metric plurality can become proxy arbitrage.

### CM7 — Metric migration
Once a metric becomes consequential, agents adapt behavior to improve the metric without improving the underlying scientific target.

Pressure:
policy changes the ecology it measures.

### CM8 — Funding-contest dissipation
Competition for a scarce scientific resource causes costly proposal/positioning effort that consumes resources otherwise available for inquiry.

Pressure:
mechanism cost includes induced strategic effort.

### CM9 — Publication-selection deterioration
A rule rewarding output/visibility selects for methods that produce more publishable results even if epistemic quality deteriorates.

Pressure:
institutional selection can favor bad search technology without explicit fraud.

### CM10 — Coalition capture
Several formally independent agents coordinate on one target/proxy and mutually validate one another.

Pressure:
headcount and nominal independence do not imply incentive independence.

### CM11 — Sybil challenge ecology
One strategic actor creates many nominal identities or organizational fronts, producing artificial “independent” support.

Pressure:
entity multiplicity is not epistemic multiplicity.

### CM12 — Performative target shift
A ranking/funding rule changes which problems are pursued, thereby changing the future distribution from which the rule is evaluated.

Pressure:
static validation can fail under endogenous distribution shift.

### CM13 — Strategic exterior-route capture
A supposedly exterior auditor receives rewards or information from the incumbent mechanism and becomes strategically dependent.

Pressure:
formal exteriority does not imply incentive exteriority.

### CM14 — Equilibrium bad science
All agents best-respond to the current reward structure and the system reaches a stable equilibrium with lower scientific quality than an available alternative mechanism.

Pressure:
equilibrium != epistemic adequacy.

### CM15 — Mechanism-switch reversal
Mechanism M1 favors challenge A; after switching incentives to M2, the same scientific agents supply challenge B and the scientific ranking reverses.

Pressure:
observed “best direction” can be mechanism-produced.

### CM16 — Counterfactual hidden challenge
A challenge absent under the incumbent mechanism appears under a randomized or differently rewarded mechanism.

Pressure:
non-observation under one institution is not evidence of nonexistence.

### CM17 — Strategic replication suppression
Replication is locally valuable but agents avoid it because rewards disproportionately favor novel positive findings.

Pressure:
option/reliability value can be institutionally under-supplied.

### CM18 — Adaptive capture of WCEPR
Agents learn the exact WCEPR admission rules and engineer packets that satisfy the receipt while preserving the same epistemic blind spot.

Pressure:
constitution observability creates an attack surface.

## Frozen primary hypotheses

### H1 — WCEPR is not incentive-robust by default
A locally valid challenge-value receipt does not guarantee robustness when agents strategically affect its inputs.

### H2 — Challenge supply is mechanism-relative
Observed candidate sets can be endogenous to incentives, funding, publication and prestige structures.

### H3 — Cost and target reports are strategic surfaces
Cost, relevance and target framing may need independent world-contact rather than being accepted as authoritative reports.

### H4 — Performative stability is not epistemic adequacy
A policy and population can reach a stable feedback equilibrium while remaining scientifically inferior to another admissible ecology.

### H5 — Strategic diversity requires incentive ancestry
Nominally distinct agents may remain common-mode under shared payoff structures.

### H6 — Mechanism counterfactuals can add scientific evidence
Changing the institutional rule can function as an intervention on the discovery ecology.

### H7 — No universally optimal scientific mechanism is licensed
Mechanism design may produce local incentive-compatible rules under declared assumptions without one institution dominating all scientific domains.

### H8 — Strategic robustness is reopenable
A constitution that agents learn to game must be capable of revising both its valuation layer and its institutional mechanism.
