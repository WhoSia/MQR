## Direct prior-art pressure

### Iterated belief revision

**Darwiche & Pearl, "On the Logic of Iterated Belief Revision", Artificial Intelligence 89 (1997), 1–29.**

Direct pressure:
- AGM-style one-step belief-set revision is too weak to regulate sequences of observations.
- Iterated revision requires an **epistemic state** richer than the current belief set.
- Revision must regulate how conditional beliefs / revision strategy itself changes over time.

MQR consequence:
- `CURRENT RELATION LABEL IS SUFFICIENT HISTORY` is not a novel target.
- MQR-4.61 cannot claim originality merely for retaining history-sensitive state.

### Dynamic revision operators

**Nayak, Pagnucco & Peppas, "Dynamic belief revision operators" (2003).**

Direct pressure:
- the revision operator itself may change after each belief-change event;
- iterated change therefore cannot be reduced to repeatedly applying one static rule without further constraints.

MQR consequence:
- `REVISION RULES CAN CHANGE` is prior art.
- MQR must instead specify what makes a constitution-level relation change admissible and auditable.

### Belief revision and scientific discovery

**Martin & Osherson, "Belief revision in the service of scientific discovery", Mathematical Social Sciences 36 (1998), 57–68.**

Direct pressure:
- belief-revision formalisms and formal theories of reliable scientific discovery have an established interface.

MQR consequence:
- applying revision vocabulary to science is not itself a contribution.

### Evidence-guided scientific belief revision

**Kuipers (2011), evidence-guided belief revision in AGM terms.**

Direct pressure:
- evidence-guided scientific change has already been explicitly connected to AGM-style revision and truth-approximation programmes.

MQR consequence:
- `EVIDENCE-DRIVEN REVISION` is prior art.

### Preference revision

**Haret & Wallner, "An AGM Approach to Revising Preferences" (2021).**

Direct pressure:
- conflict among preference comparisons can be revised by formal postulates;
- revision can be rationalized through structured priority over comparisons rather than an undifferentiated scalar.

MQR consequence:
- revising relational order structures is not novel.
- MQR-4.61 cannot redescribe generic preference revision as its invention.

### Real options, sequential R&D and path dependency

**Koussis, Martzoukos & Trigeorgis, "Real R&D options with time-to-learn and learning-by-doing", Annals of Operations Research 151 (2007), 29–55.**

Direct pressure:
- optional and sequential R&D actions can have timing flexibility;
- history of decisions can change later decisions and induce path dependence;
- reversibility and learning can affect the value of staged action.

MQR consequence:
- option value, irreversibility and history-sensitive R&D decisions are prior art.
- `LOST OPTION` is not a new economic concept.

### Path dependence is more specific than "history matters"

**Vergne & Durand, "The missing link between the theory and empirics of path dependence" (2010)** and related 2011 work.

Direct pressure:
- path dependence must be distinguished from generic claims that history matters;
- process, outcome/lock-in and causal mechanism should not be conflated;
- empirical/counterfactual discipline is required.

MQR consequence:
- MQR-4.61 must reject chronology-only or sunk-cost path sensitivity.
- path effects require a typed, material state-bearing mechanism.

## Internal MQR pressure

MQR-4.53 already rejected a universal scalar progress measure and preserved only local structures.

MQR-4.59 separated promotion governance from a universal definition of scientific progress.

MQR-4.60 established:
- pairwise dominance != total order;
- Pareto non-domination != unique promotion;
- local veto != universal priority;
- duplicate/common-mode reasons != independent warrant;
- scope/horizon reversal can be legitimate;

Therefore MQR-4.61 may not reintroduce a scalar through the back door by assigning one "history value" to a transition path.

## Novelty kill

MQR-4.61 claims **no invention** of:
- iterated belief revision;
- epistemic-state dependence;
- dynamic revision operators;
- nonmonotonic belief change;
- belief revision in science;
- preference revision;
- path dependence;
- hysteresis as a general phenomenon;
- irreversibility / lock-in;
- real-option value;
- sequential R&D learning;
- state augmentation to summarize relevant history.
