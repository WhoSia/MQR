## 1. Rigorization of calculus

### Judith V. Grabiner
- “Who Gave You the Epsilon? The Origins of Cauchy's Rigorous Calculus,” *American Mathematical Monthly* 90(3), 1983, 185–194.
- related work on the pre-history of delta–epsilon methods.

Pressure:
the development of rigorous analysis is historically extended rather than a one-step replacement of intuition by formalism. The useful MQR reading is that proof standards moved the frontier of what could remain tacit or “obvious.”

### Alexandre Borovik & Mikhail Katz
- “Who Gave You the Cauchy–Weierstrass Tale? The Dual History of Rigorous Calculus.”
- DOI: `10.1007/s10699-011-9235-x`.

Pressure:
avoid retrojecting one later Weierstrassian standard as the unique historical meaning of rigor.

## 2. Rigor and actual mathematical practice

### Oliver Tatton-Brown
- “Rigour and Intuition,” *Erkenntnis* 86, 1757–1781.
- DOI: `10.1007/s10670-019-00180-9`.

Pressure:
rigorous proof can contain high-level compressed inference when nontrivial steps are supportable by more detail. Mathematical intuition can remain research-guiding without becoming proof.

### Jeremy Avigad
- “Understanding Proofs.”

Pressure:
proof understanding involves high-level organization and inferential recognition, not merely verification of a maximally expanded derivation.

### Silvia De Toffoli & Valeria Giardino
- “Forms and Roles of Diagrams in Knot Theory.”
- DOI: `10.1007/s10670-013-9568-7`.

Pressure:
expert diagrammatic manipulation can participate in mathematical discovery and evidential reasoning.

### Georg Kreisel
- “Informal Rigour and Completeness Proofs.”
- DOI: `10.1016/S0049-237X(08)71525-8`.

Pressure:
informal rigor is a historically important alternative to collapsing disciplined mathematical understanding into one fixed low-level formalization.

## 3. Scientific discovery and model-based reasoning

### Herbert A. Simon
- “Does Scientific Discovery Have a Logic?” *Philosophy of Science* 40(4), 1973.
- DOI: `10.1086/288559`.

Pressure:
discovery can contain normative heuristics/search procedures without reducing to deductive proof or requiring mystical creativity.

### Nancy J. Nersessian
- work on model-based reasoning and distributed cognitive systems.

Pressure:
scientific reasoning includes constructing, manipulating and revising mental, computational and physical models. Consequence generation can therefore precede complete proposition-level formalization.

## 4. Local warrant

### John D. Norton
- “A Material Theory of Induction,” *Philosophy of Science* 70, 2003, 647–670.
- DOI: `10.1086/378858`.

Pressure:
inductive warrant can be supplied by domain-specific material facts rather than one universal formal schema. MQR-4.55 treats this as strong pressure against a universal scientific consequence generator.

## 5. Tacit competence

### H. M. Collins
- “Tacit Knowledge, Trust and the Q of Sapphire,” *Social Studies of Science* 31(1), 2001.
- DOI: `10.1177/030631201031001004`.

Pressure:
written protocol may not preserve all competence needed for replication.

MQR boundary:
`PROTOCOL != CAPABILITY`, but also `TACIT != INEFFABLE AUTHORITY`.

## 6. Background assumptions, criticism and trained judgment

Longino-style contextual empiricism pressures the independence of evidence from background assumptions.

Daston & Galison's historical account of objectivity includes **trained judgment** rather than a story in which mature science simply eliminates skilled perception.

Leonelli's work on epistemic diversity pressures universalized standardized repertoires that can suppress locally relevant forms of inquiry.

## 9. Novelty kill

MQR-4.55 does not claim to invent:
- rigor/intuition distinctions;
- calculus rigorization;
- informal or high-level mathematical reasoning;
- diagrammatic/model-based scientific reasoning;
- scientific discovery heuristics;
- tacit knowledge;
- trained judgment;
- contextual criticism;
- epistemic diversity;
- open-ended hypothesis/rival search.
